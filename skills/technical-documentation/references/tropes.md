# General LLM Prose Tropes

Use this reference after semantic and temporal correctness are established. It covers language-independent prose habits that often add volume without adding technical meaning.

These are review signals, not a blacklist. Keep a pattern when it performs a necessary function for the reader.

## Repeated framing

Remove repetitions such as:

- a heading restated by its first sentence;
- an introduction that previews an already obvious structure;
- a paragraph followed by “in other words” with no new clarification;
- a section conclusion repeated at the start of the next section;
- the same fact repeated in introduction, body, summary, and conclusion.

Repeat information only when a different context, risk, or reader action justifies it.

## Meta narration

Avoid narrating the document when its structure already communicates the same information.

Examples include announcing that the document, section, or following paragraphs will explain or examine the topic. State the scope explicitly only when the boundary itself matters.

## Inflated evaluation

Do not add unsupported descriptions such as:

- robust;
- powerful;
- seamless;
- flexible;
- intuitive;
- efficient;
- comprehensive;
- optimized;
- enhanced.

Replace evaluation with an observable property, constraint, measurement, or consequence. Remove it when no technical claim remains.

## Abstract filler

Prefer concrete subjects, actions, conditions, and outcomes over vague references to aspects, dimensions, elements, approaches, capabilities, or considerations.

Do not use abstraction to conceal an unknown actor, unclear ownership, or missing mechanism.

## Mechanical transitions

Do not begin every paragraph with a discourse marker or mechanically connect sentences with words equivalent to “also”, “therefore”, “notably”, “meanwhile”, or “as a result”.

Keep a transition when it expresses a real logical relationship that would otherwise be unclear.

## Formulaic contrast and emphasis

Avoid repeated templates such as:

- “not merely X, but Y”;
- “it is important to note”;
- “the key point is”;
- “at its core”;
- “this ensures that” without a demonstrated causal link.

State the actual distinction, importance, or effect directly.

## Symmetry and list inflation

Do not force content into matching three-part lists, repeated heading templates, or exhaustive-looking categories merely for rhetorical symmetry.

Use a list when its items form a real set and parallel structure improves retrieval. Do not add weak items to make the list look complete.

## Empty summaries and conclusions

Do not append a summary that only repeats a short document or section. A conclusion should supply a decision, implication, limitation, or next action that the preceding text has not already made sufficiently clear.

## Audit

For each paragraph, ask:

1. What new fact, instruction, rationale, or constraint does it add?
2. Does it repeat its heading or neighboring text?
3. Does it contain an evaluation without evidence?
4. Could a concrete actor or operation replace an abstraction?
5. Is the transition logically necessary?
6. Is the structure driven by the subject or by a writing template?
7. Would deleting the sentence change the reader's model or action?

Remove prose that survives none of these questions.
